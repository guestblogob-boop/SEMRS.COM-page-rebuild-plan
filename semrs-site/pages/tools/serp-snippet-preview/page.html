What a SERP snippet is and why it matters

A SERP snippet is the block of text Google shows for your page in search results: the site name and URL path, the blue clickable title and a short description underneath. It is the first thing a searcher sees from your business, often before they have heard of you. Two pages can rank in the same position and get very different numbers of clicks purely because one snippet is clearer, more specific and more relevant to the search.

Google builds the title from your title tag in most cases and usually takes the description from your meta description, but it can rewrite either one when it thinks another part of the page answers the search better. You cannot force Google to use your text. What you can do is write a title and description that are accurate, specific and the right length, which makes Google far more likely to keep them.

Why pixel width matters more than character count

Google does not cut titles at a fixed number of characters. It cuts them when they run out of horizontal space, which on desktop is roughly 600 pixels for the title. A title full of wide letters such as W and M fills that space faster than one with narrow letters such as i and l. That is why two 60-character titles can behave differently, and why this tool shows both the character count and an approximate pixel width for each field.

Descriptions work the same way. On desktop, about 920 pixels of description text (roughly two lines) is a safe target. Mobile results show a similar amount but wrap differently, so the tool shows both versions.

How to use the SERP preview tool

  1. Paste your page title into the Page title box. Watch the counter: green means the length is in a healthy range; red means Google will probably cut it off.
  2. Paste your meta description. Aim for a full sentence or two that tells the searcher exactly what they will get.
  3. Add the page URL so the breadcrumb path looks the way it will in Google.
  4. Type the search phrase you expect people to use. Google bolds matching words in descriptions, and the preview does the same, which shows whether your copy actually matches the search.
  5. Tick Show a date if your page is an article that Google is likely to date, so you can see how much space the date takes.
  6. Click Copy HTML tags and paste the result into your page, or copy the title and description into your SEO plugin.

Your last entries are saved in your browser, so you can come back and keep editing. Nothing is sent to a server.

Worked example

A plumbing company in Austin starts with this title: "Home | Johnson Plumbing Services LLC – Plumbing, Heating, Drains, Water Heaters and More". In the preview the title is cut off after "Water Heaters and…", and the first word a searcher reads is "Home", which says nothing.

A better version: "Emergency Plumber in Austin, TX – 24/7, Licensed | Johnson Plumbing". It fits within the pixel limit, leads with what the searcher wants, includes the city, adds two reasons to click and keeps the brand at the end. The description then supports it: "Burst pipe or no hot water? Licensed Austin plumbers on call 24/7. Upfront prices, no call-out fee on weekdays. Call or book online in 60 seconds."

Best practices for titles and descriptions

  • Put the most important words first. People scan the left side of results, and truncation removes the end.
  • Write one unique title and description for every page. Duplicates make pages compete with each other and give Google no reason to prefer either.
  • Match the search intent. A "how to" search wants a guide; a "near me" search wants a local business with clear contact options.
  • Keep the brand short and at the end of the title, except on your homepage.
  • Use numbers and specifics where they are true: prices from, opening hours, service area, years in business.
  • Write descriptions as a promise the page keeps. If the page does not deliver what the snippet says, visitors leave quickly.

Common mistakes

  • Stuffing several keyword variations into one title ("Plumber Austin, Austin Plumber, Plumbers in Austin"). It reads as spam and Google often rewrites it.
  • Leaving default titles such as "Home", "Services" or "Untitled page".
  • Writing descriptions under about 70 characters, which gives Google too little to work with.
  • Using ALL CAPS or lots of symbols to stand out. It lowers trust and is often rewritten.
  • Promising things the page does not mention, such as a discount that is not on the page.

Tips for local businesses

If you serve customers in a specific area in the US, UK, Canada or Australia, include the main city or region in the title of your service pages, written the way locals search ("Plumber in Leeds", "Dentist in Toronto"). Do not create near-identical pages for dozens of towns you do not really serve; Google treats those as doorway pages. Your homepage title can carry your main city, while each genuine location page gets its own title with its own address details. Keep the business name, address and phone number consistent with your Google Business Profile.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a title tag be?

There is no exact character limit because Google measures pixels. Around 50–60 characters, or under about 600 pixels on desktop, usually displays in full. Shorter is fine if it is specific.

How long should a meta description be?

Roughly 120–155 characters is a reliable target. Google may show more or less depending on the device and the search, and it sometimes writes its own description from your page content.

Why does Google show a different title from the one I wrote?

Google rewrites titles that are too long, stuffed with keywords, too generic or not matching the page content. Writing an accurate, specific title of a sensible length is the best way to keep yours.

Do title and description changes affect rankings?

The title is a relevance signal, so a clearer title can help. The meta description is not a ranking factor, but a better description can increase the number of people who click your result.

Is this preview exactly what Google will show?

It is a close approximation using the same fonts and typical limits. Google tests layouts constantly and adds extra elements such as images, ratings or site links for some results, so treat the preview as a strong guide rather than a guarantee.
